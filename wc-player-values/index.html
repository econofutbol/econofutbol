From €50m to €200m in Three Editions.

At the 2006 World Cup, Ronaldinho — the world's best player — was worth €50m. By 2018, Mbappé arrived at €200m aged 19. Does the most expensive player at the World Cup ever justify their price tag?

Zero. That's how many players worth €100m or more lined up at the 2006 World Cup. At the 2026 edition, there are 18. Across six tournaments, player market valuations have inflated far faster than transfer fees — yet the question has remained the same: does the most expensive talent actually deliver when it matters most?

Germany 2006: The Pre-Inflation Era

The most expensive player in Germany was Ronaldinho, valued at exactly €50m — but Brazil's run ended in the quarter-finals. Ivory Coast's Didier Drogba (€38m) didn't even survive the group stage.

The actual champions, Italy, barely register on this list. Gianluigi Buffon, Alessandro Nesta and Francesco Totti were valued at just €35m each — among the cheapest names here, and the only ones who lifted the trophy.

South Africa 2010: Ronaldo Approaches the Ceiling

Cristiano Ronaldo led the valuations at €90m — the first player to approach six figures. Portugal didn't make it past the round of 16.

The five players who actually lifted the trophy wore Spain's colors — Iniesta, Fàbregas, Xavi, Villa, Torres — and none of them cost more than €60m.

Brazil 2014: The First Members

Two players crossed €100m for the first time at a World Cup: Cristiano Ronaldo and Lionel Messi, both at €120m. Their tournaments split immediately — Portugal crashed out in the group stage, while Messi's Argentina finished runners-up.

The trophy went to Germany, where four champions — Müller, Kroos, Götze and Reus — cost less than half of Ronaldo and Messi's price tag.

Russia 2018: The Year Everything Changed

Twelve players arrived at €100m or more. Kylian Mbappé, aged 19, led the field at €200m — the first player to reach that threshold — and delivered: four goals and a winner's medal.

The two biggest names of the previous decade didn't fare as well. Messi (€120m) and Ronaldo (€100m) were both out by the round of 16.

Qatar 2022: A Post-COVID Reset

Just six players carried €100m+ valuations — a post-COVID dip from 2018's twelve. Mbappé, priciest at €180m, nearly went all the way: 8 goals, the Golden Boot, runner-up.

England supplied six of the fifteen names here — Bellingham, Saka, Foden, Rice, Kane, Rashford — and all six stopped in the same place: the quarter-finals. Germany's Jamal Musiala, valued at €110m, didn't escape the group stage. Argentina, the eventual champions, don't have a single player on this list.

USA/Canada/Mexico 2026: 18 Players at €100m+

18 players carry price tags of €100m or more. Lamine Yamal and Erling Haaland jointly lead the valuation table at €200m — the first players other than Mbappé to reach that ceiling since he set it in 2018.

Unlike every edition before it, these bars can't yet be colored by the rounds these players will reach — that story hasn't been written. Five players are worth €150m or more. Whether the price tags are justified is still an open question.

The 100m Club

How the market's most expensive tier grew from two players to eighteen.

■ Earlier editions ■ WC 2014

The First Members

From 2006 to 2014, the €100m player didn't exist at the World Cup. The bar showed a flat zero. Then in Brazil, Messi and Ronaldo crossed it together — just two players in the entire field.

It felt exclusive. It wouldn't stay that way for long.

The Explosion

Russia 2018 changed everything. Twelve players at €100m or more — six times the count of 2014. Mbappé set a ceiling at €200m that wouldn't be matched until 2026.

The market had found a new gear. And it hasn't slowed down.

The New Normal

At 2026, 18 players carry €100m+ price tags — three times the 2018 count. For the first time, the most expensive player in the tournament isn't Mbappé: Lamine Yamal (17 years old) and Erling Haaland both carry €200m valuations.

The €100m player is no longer a rarity. The question is whether they justify the investment.

Where the Money Goes

How the game's value splits across positions — and how the total has ballooned.

The Position Split

Attackers have claimed the largest share of squad value at every World Cup since 2006 — never below 40%.

Goalkeepers have never broken 8%, and in 2026 their share has shrunk to just 4.4%, the lowest on record.

Despite squad value growing nearly fourfold, this split has barely moved — every position has stayed within a tight few points of its 2006 share.

The Money Pile

Total squad value has nearly quadrupled in twenty years — from €4.5bn in 2006 to €17.2bn in 2026.

Attackers alone are now worth more than every position combined at the 2006 World Cup.

The shares barely moved — but the money behind them didn't. Every position is worth at least 2.5 times what it was in 2006.

The Scatter

Every outfield player in a 2026 squad, placed at their peak World Cup value — their highest valuation at any edition — against their World Cup output from 2006 to 2022: goals plus assists, across every tournament they've actually played. 2026's own results aren't in this dataset yet, so it carries no output of its own.

If the price tag tracked proven output, the dots would climb left to right. They don't.

The Deliverers

Two names pull away. Lionel Messi's World Cup value peaked at just €120m yet he returned 21 goals and assists across five World Cups. Kylian Mbappé sits top-right: €200m, tied for the highest World Cup value on record, already at 14 aged 23.

Cristiano Ronaldo (€120m, 10) and Neymar (€150m, 11) complete the elite — the rare cases where the price was roughly paid.

Paid, Not Delivered

Then the other column. Erling Haaland and Lamine Yamal are both valued at €200m — the joint-highest World Cup price tags on record — with zero career World Cup goals or assists on record — neither had a World Cup appearance before 2026, and this dataset doesn't include 2026 output yet.

Pedri, Vitinha and Declan Rice (€150m, €140m, €120m) make the harder case: real minutes, real opportunity, inside an actual World Cup squad — and zero return all the same.

Price Doesn't Buy Production

Peak World Cup value against output per 90 minutes, every player since 2006.

The Overachievers

Start in the top-left corner — cheap tags, elite rates. Álvaro Morata (€50m) posts the best rate in the entire dataset: 1.95 goals and assists per 90. James Rodríguez (€50m, 1.63) and André Schürrle (just €30m, 1.45) aren't far behind.

None of the three ever carried a World Cup price tag above €50m.

The Deliverers

Move toward the middle-right, and the price tags climb — but so does the output. Kylian Mbappé pairs a World Cup value tied for the highest on record (€200m) with 1.12 per 90. Neymar (€150m, 0.84), Vinícius Jr. (€140m, 0.90) and Lionel Messi (€120m, 0.82) sit right behind him.

These are the rare cases where an elite price bought an elite rate. Cristiano Ronaldo shows it's not automatic — the same €120m as Messi, but just 0.51 per 90.

The Underachievers

Then the bottom-right. Declan Rice (€120m) played 417 minutes at a World Cup and returned 0.00 goals and assists per 90. Vitinha (€140m) matched that rate in a shorter run — 120 minutes, nothing to show for it.

Same price range as the deliverers. None of the output.

2026: The Most Expensive Tournament Ever

The 30 most valuable individual players at this year's World Cup — ranked by price, not yet by performance.

The data tells a clear story about squads: richer teams go further. But at the individual level, the picture is murkier. The most expensive player rarely becomes the most impactful one. Mbappé is the exception — twice the most expensive, twice the top scorer.

In 2026, the field features €200m players who haven't yet proved themselves at a senior World Cup. Lamine Yamal at 17. Haaland, who has never played a World Cup knockout match. The price tags are extraordinary. What happens on the pitch will be, too.
