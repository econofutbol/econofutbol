A World Cup Isn't a Shop Window. It's an Audit.

Everyone believes a great tournament makes a player. Five editions of Transfermarkt data say the opposite – the market had already made up its mind.

A brilliant summer, the story goes, sends a player's price soaring. And the numbers seem to prove it: players 22 and under leave a World Cup worth 24% more, while the over-32s lose a fifth of their value. A perfect tale of breakout kids and fading veterans. There is only one problem – it was going to happen anyway. Follow the same players through the ordinary summers either side of the tournament, and almost the entire effect disappears.

A World Cup Is a Shop Window for the Young

It is the most repeated line in football: play well at a World Cup while you're young, and the money follows. Scouts are watching, the whole planet is watching, and a good three weeks can define a career.

Line players up by age and the market seems to agree completely – the young climb, the old fall. The shape is exactly the story everyone tells.

Kids Gain a Quarter. Veterans Lose a Fifth.

Put numbers on it and it only gets more convincing. The players 22 and under walk away worth +24%. The 32-and-overs lose −20%. A clean, monotonic slide from young and rising to old and falling.

If you stopped here, you'd write the obvious headline: the World Cup is where the next generation gets paid. So let's not stop here.

But a 21-Year-Old Was Rising Anyway

A young player's value was already climbing before a ball was kicked; a veteran's was already sliding. So ask the only question that matters: did the tournament change what would have happened regardless?

Measure each player against his own ordinary summers – the same calendar window, one and two years either side – and the age pattern doesn't shrink. It collapses. Every bar flattens to zero. The under-22s even come out slightly negative: they gained less in a World Cup summer than in a normal one.

There Is No World Cup Bump – Just a Career Arc

Zoom all the way out. Take the same players and measure the identical window shifted across seven summers, from three years before to three years after. The share whose value rises falls steadily as they age – from 55% to 13% – one smooth curve.

The World Cup summer sits right on it. 36.4% of players gained; the two neighbouring summers alone predicted 35.8%. The gap is statistical noise – a paired test returns p = 0.94. The tournament didn't bend the curve. It was never the story; age was.

One Thing Survives the Control: Scoring

Maybe it was the transfer, then – the big move a good tournament triggers. But a World Cup summer is no busier for changing club than any other (35% vs 33–39% either side), and movers beat their own trend by exactly as much as the players who stayed: nothing.

Only one thing clears the control. Not age, not the move – producing. A player with 4+ goals or assists beats his own trajectory by ~18%, a median €6.1m more than an ordinary summer would have paid. And it pays in euros, not just percentages.

Scoring Pays in Every Bracket – But the Top Is a Two-Sided Bet

It's tempting to say the money flows to the already-rich: among players whose value rose, starting price tracks the euro gain at a striking +0.75. But that correlation is a mirage – keep only the winners and an expensive player's gain is large by construction. Across everyone, the link is zero.

Subtract the trend and the picture inverts: the goal-contribution gradient is real in every price tier, and in percentage terms the mid-market gains most (€2–5m: +19%). What sets the €15m+ names apart is the downside – a big-money forward who scores nothing loses €1m against his own trend. At the top, a World Cup is a bet, not a gift.

So What Does It Mean for 2026?

One rule survived the audit – scoring. Point it at the players who just finished the tournament.

A World Cup can't be measured against 2026 values yet: the market takes a median of five months to react. But the pattern from five past editions can be pointed forward – carefully, as odds rather than promises.

Better Production, Better Odds – but Only Odds

So take the one rule that survived the audit and turn it forward. Forwards are priced on goal contributions, defenders and midfielders on minutes – so those are the two tracks. And the odds line up cleanly: the more a player produced, the more often he beat his own trend. A forward with 4+ G+A did it 72% of the time; a defender or midfielder with 450+ minutes, 69% – both well clear of the coin-flip line.

But 72% is not 100%. Even in the strongest group, nearly a quarter of players still fell short of their own trend, and fitting on four editions to rank the fifth barely beats a coin flip out of sample. So what follows is a watchlist, not a forecast – a bet with its historical odds printed on the ticket.

So a great World Cup doesn't reveal a player's value. It confirms what the market had already decided – priced in months before kickoff, in the slow climb and fall of a career. The tournament isn't a shop window where unknowns get discovered. It's an audit that mostly ratifies the existing books.

The one line item that survives the audit is production. Score, create, and the market pays you – in euros, in every price bracket, above and beyond what your career was already doing. Everything else – the age, the move, the three magic weeks – was going to happen anyway.

Which is why the 2026 list is a watchlist and not a set of predictions. Mbappé at 14 goal contributions, Cubarsí playing every minute at 19 – the pattern favours them. But a quarter of players just like them still fall short, and Messi's sky-high percentage upside is really an artefact of a €15m tag at 38. Come back when the window closes and we'll score the ticket. That's the honest way to bet.
