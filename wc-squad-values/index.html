Five World Cups. 160 Squads. Billions on the Pitch.

Five World Cups, 160 squads, one question: does the money decide who lifts the trophy?

Morocco were ranked 17th by squad value at the 2022 World Cup. They reached the semi-finals. Germany were ranked 6th. They went home in the group stage. So does the money matter? Across 160 squads and five editions, the answer is: yes, almost perfectly. And also: not at all. Here's why both are true.

Germany 2006: Italy Win as Favourites

Italy won ranked 1st by squad value — the most expensive squad took the trophy. France, ranked 5th, reached the final before Zidane's headbutt changed the result. Germany, the hosts, finished third ranked 9th.

Brazil, ranked 2nd, and England, ranked 3rd, both went out in the quarter-finals.

South Africa 2010: The Favourites Deliver

Spain entered ranked 1st and won the tournament — the most expensive squad took the trophy. France, ranked 3rd, and Italy, ranked 5th, both went home in the group stage. Uruguay, ranked 13th, reached the semi-finals.

The pattern held at the very top; everything below that threshold was unpredictable.

Brazil 2014: The Blueprint Works

Germany entered ranked 2nd. They beat Portugal 4–0, Brazil 7–1, and lifted the trophy in Rio. Spain went out in the group stage ranked 1st.

But Costa Rica reached the quarter-finals ranked 29th. Even in the most justified edition, the exceptions were extreme.

Russia 2018: France Win, Germany Crash

France entered ranked 1st and won the tournament. Germany, ranked 3rd, lost to Mexico, drew with Sweden, and went home in the group stage. Croatia, ranked 9th, played the final.

Expectations built on squad value can collapse spectacularly.

Qatar 2022: When the Script Breaks

Germany entered ranked 6th and went home in the group stage alongside Belgium, ranked 9th. Morocco entered ranked 17th and reached the semi-finals. Argentina, ranked 7th, won the trophy.

No edition since 2006 had produced more noise around the signal.

2026: The Most Expensive Field Ever

48 squads for the first time. France, England, Spain, Portugal, and Germany all arrive with squads valued above a billion euros. The expanded format guarantees that at least two of those favourites exit in the pool stages.

History says the pattern holds at the top. History also says someone always breaks it.

The Money-Results Link

Five editions, 160 teams, all on one chart. The wealthiest squads cluster at the top — the pattern is visible. But nothing is guaranteed.

Morocco 2022 reached the semi-finals ranked 17th by squad value. Germany 2018 went home in the group stage ranked 3rd. So is this a real pattern — or just noise?

The Staircase

It's a real pattern. Median squad value nearly doubles between group stage exits and semi-finalists — and doubles again for winners.

All five World Cup winners since 2006 ranked in the top 7 by squad value. Three of those five won as the most expensive squad in the tournament. The correlation is real, consistent, and has held across every edition.

The Road to the Trophy

Five champions, four opponents each — did the money advantage hold?

Every winner had to survive the group stage and beat four knockout opponents to lift the trophy. The staircase shows the aggregate pattern. But at the individual level, the story is more interesting.

Italy 2006: The Favourites Delivered

Italy entered ranked 1st by squad value and lifted the trophy. Every opponent they faced was cheaper. Germany in the semi-finals (€203m vs Italy's €550m) was the closest contest on paper — and even then, Italy had more than twice the firepower.

The trophy went exactly where the money said it should.

Spain 2010: The Richest Prevailed

Spain entered ranked 1st and won. Portugal in the round of 16 (€433m) was competitive on value. Paraguay in the quarter-finals (€76m) wasn't — yet nearly held on until penalties. The pattern mostly held; the exceptions just made it interesting.

Germany 2014: Every Match Was Winnable

Germany entered ranked 2nd. They faced Algeria (€84m), then France (€506m), then Brazil — a team worth 87% of Germany's squad value — and beat them 7–1. Argentina in the Final was worth €576m. The score was 1–0 after extra time. The value gap didn't determine the margin.

France 2018: The Richest Road

France entered ranked 1st — the most expensive squad in the tournament. Every opponent was cheaper. Belgium in the semi-finals (€977m) was the most expensive opponent any champion has faced at that stage across five editions. France had more. They always had more.

Argentina 2022: The Exception

Argentina (€836m) faced France (€1,598m) in the Final — an opponent worth nearly double their squad value. It was the only time in five editions that a champion had to beat a richer team to lift the trophy.

Argentina won on penalties. The script doesn't always hold.

The Outliers

Who defied — and who confirmed — the money script?

The aggregate pattern is clear. But football's best stories live in the exceptions — the overachievers who ran deep on a fraction of the squad value, and the Golden Generations that went home early.

The Pattern Has Two Sides

The richest squads are expected to deliver. Spain 2014, Germany 2018, and Belgium 2022 all arrived as top-5 squads by value and went home in the group stage. The squad value was there. The result wasn't.

At the other end: Costa Rica 2014, Australia 2022, and Ecuador 2006 all reached the knockout stages as some of the cheapest squads in their tournament. Are those flukes — or is something else going on?

Who Defied the Script?

Each team gets two ranks within their own tournament — one for squad value, one for round reached. The gap is the difference: positive means you went further than your squad's market value suggested; negative means you fell short.

Costa Rica 2014 tops this measure: a quarter-final from the 4th-cheapest squad in the tournament. Morocco 2022 went further in absolute terms — a semi-final — but from a higher base. At the other end: Germany 2018 (ranked 3rd) and Belgium 2022 (ranked 9th) — group stage exits from squads worth close to a billion euros.

Are these one-off shocks — or is there a pattern for certain nations?

The Expensive Disappointments

England, Germany, Spain, Portugal, Belgium. Five nations that arrive at every World Cup among the richest squads — and consistently fail to match their market value. The ✕ marks a group stage exit: the most expensive failure in football.

Belgium's Golden Generation won nothing. England haven't reached a World Cup final since 1966. But on the other side of the chart, who keeps getting more out of less?

Punching Above Their Weight

Croatia arrived at the 2018 World Cup as the 9th-richest squad. They played the final. In 2022 they were 12th. They played the semi-finals. Japan, South Korea, and Morocco have all made knockout rounds with squads worth a fraction of the European elite.

In 2026, the field is the most expensive ever assembled. So who should we be watching?

Money matters at the World Cup — but it doesn't decide the trophy. The correlation is real and consistent across all five editions, yet weak enough that almost every tournament throws up at least one team that defies it completely.

The one thing money guarantees: every winner since 2006 came from the top 7 by squad value. No one has won from outside it. Reaching the final is less certain — Croatia and the Netherlands both did it ranked 9th. Spain 2014 proves the ceiling — ranked 1st, eliminated in the group stage. Morocco 2022 proves the floor doesn't exist.

In 2026, the field is the most expensive ever assembled. France, England, Spain, Portugal, and Germany arrive with billion-euro squads and decades of World Cup disappointment between them. History says at least one will break the pattern. History also says most of them won't.
