A World Cup Doesn't Heat Up the Transfer Market. It Taxes the Players Who Were There.

The window after a tournament pays the same premium as any other. The difference is who gets the big cheque – and how little it depends on what they actually did.

Every four years the story repeats: a player has a big summer and, within weeks, a giant club pays a fee that dwarfs what he was worth before kickoff. James Rodríguez to Real Madrid for €75m after eight goal contributions in 2014; Ángel Di María to the same club in 2010 with zero. It looks like a World Cup gold rush. It isn't – the window after a tournament is no richer than any other. The tax hides inside the players themselves.

A World Cup Doesn't Move the Market

The belief is a post-tournament spending spree – clubs dazzled by three weeks of football, throwing money around. So take every paid signing into a big-five club since 2006 and split it in two: the six windows that open right after a World Cup, and every other window.

The premium clubs pay over a player's market value is identical – a median of 1.0x in both, the same long tail of overpays. A World Cup doesn't heat the market by a single degree.

But the World Cup Players Are the Big Cheques

The window is ordinary. Who gets bought is not. The typical post-tournament signing costs about what any signing costs – €3.2m against €3.6m. But the players who were actually at the World Cup go for €6.7m, nearly double – at that same unremarkable premium.

So a World Cup doesn't inflate the market. It just decides who gets the big cheque – and the biggest cheques, edition by edition, tell one story.

The World Cup Tax, Window by Window

That doubling never spreads evenly – each window it lands on a handful of names. We measure the overpay – our World Cup tax – as the fee a club paid minus what the player was worth the week before the tournament kicked off. Here are the biggest, one edition at a time, from 2006 to the live 2026 market.

The First €21m Overpay

It starts quietly. Michael Carrick has a competent, unspectacular tournament, and a €6m midfielder leaves for €27m – a €21m tax, the first of its kind.

€33m for Zero Goal Contributions

Four years on, it has a face. Ángel Di María plays the whole 2010 World Cup with zero goal contributions, and Real Madrid pays €33m for a €10m winger. The fee has already come loose from the football.

Real Madrid Pays Twice

Then it doubles – same club, same profile. James Rodríguez earns his €40m tax with eight goal contributions. But the same summer, Di María leaves for €75m on one. The earned and the unearned, side by side.

Both €60m, Earned or Not

The gap only sharpens. Kylian Mbappé is real football – four goal contributions, a World Cup won. Beside him, a goalkeeper, Kepa, carries the identical €60m tax. The market can't tell them apart.

The Biggest Tax Ever

And it keeps climbing. Enzo Fernández is worth €35m before Qatar and €121m after – an €86m tax, the biggest ever recorded, on a name barely rated a month earlier.

Same Tax, Live in 2026

Nothing has changed. As of 29 July 2026, the live window is taxing its class exactly like every one before it: Anderson (+€60m) and Rogers (+€48m), names the market had already made up its mind about.

But Does It Follow the Football?

Six windows of headline names make the tax easy to feel – and easy to cherry-pick. The honest test is the full sample: every big post-tournament buy since 2006, each player's overpay set against how he actually played at the World Cup.

The Fee Barely Follows the Football

It barely does. If clubs were buying form, the overpay would climb with performance. Instead the correlation is a limp +0.14 – and some of the largest taxes went to players who finished near the bottom of the tournament.

A brilliant World Cup and a forgettable one command about the same premium. The fee is deaf to the football.

The Priciest Forwards Did the Least

Split out the forwards clubs overpaid for, and the pattern sharpens into names. The priciest managed one goal contribution apiece – Rogers, Di María, Ramos – each a €44–48m tax on a near-silent month.

The lone exception is James Rodríguez: eight goal contributions, and the only forward whose football came close to earning his fee.

Minutes Don't Explain It Either

Defenders and midfielders are bought on minutes, not goals – and it lands the same way. Enzo Fernández and Anderson at least played deep into their tournaments; plenty who barely featured were taxed just as hard.

How much a club overpaid says almost nothing about how the player's tournament went. So if it isn't the football – what is everyone paying for?

Sometimes the Bet Pays

The tax isn't always wasted. Di María joined Real Madrid on zero goal contributions in 2010, gave them four seasons, and was sold for €75m – more than double what they paid.

But that is exactly what it is: a bet on the name, not the numbers. At the moment of paying, no club can tell a future mainstay from a €60m mistake. It only knows the name.

Who Writes the Cheques

It isn't one reckless club – it's the whole elite, every tournament. Chelsea have spent €570m on World Cup players across all six windows; Barcelona, Liverpool and Real Madrid each over €360m. The colours show it repeating, edition after edition. The World Cup tax isn't a mistake anyone learns from – it's the cost of doing business at the top.

So a great World Cup doesn't earn a player his move. Being at one does. The window afterwards is no richer than any other, the premium no steeper – but the players who were there cost nearly double, and the fee barely flinches at whether they scored eight or zero.

What clubs are buying isn't the football. It's the three weeks of attention – the name that just spent a month on every screen on earth. James Rodríguez earned his €75m. Di María, one goal contribution later, cost the same. The market paid for the World Cup, not the player.

It has done so for twenty years, and it did it again in 2026. The tax is baked in – the only thing that changes is whose name is on the cheque.
